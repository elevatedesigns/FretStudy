# LEVEL 3 — Rhythm & Reading
**Big goal:** Sound like a real rhythm guitarist (strum patterns, bass-strum, 7th and sus chords), know every note in first position, and understand why chords group into families (I–IV–V).
**Time:** 4–6 weeks · **Level Song:** "When the Saints Go Marching In" (G, G7, C, D)

---

### L3.1 — Your First Strum Pattern: "Old Faithful"
**Thread:** Rhythm · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L2.6

**Goal:** Play the most common pop/folk strum pattern: **D D U U D U**.

**Explain it like this:**
> Keep your hand swinging down-up like always. But on beat 3, **miss the strings on purpose** on the way down (a "ghost strum") and then catch them on the way up. Your hand never stops. You're just choosing when to touch the strings. That skip makes the rhythm bounce!

**Must-know facts:**
- Count: `1  2 &  (3) &  4 &` → **D  D U  (ghost) U  D U**.
- The hand pattern is still steady down-up eighth notes (L2.6). Only *contact* changes.
- The upstroke on the "&" of 2 that carries over the missing beat-3 strum is a **syncopation** (a sound "off" the main beat).

**Graphics:**
- Strum Grid with 8 boxes: solid ↓ on 1, solid ↓↑ on 2&, a **faded ghost ↓** on 3, solid ↑ on 3&, solid ↓↑ on 4&.
- A side-view animation: the pick passes *above* the strings on the ghost strum.

**Try it:** Muted "chk" strums first, then Em, then `G | Em | C | D`.
**Check it:** 4 bars at 80 BPM, pattern detected ≥ 80%.

### L3.2 — The Boom-Chuck: Bass Note + Strum
**Thread:** Rhythm/Technique · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L3.1, L1.6

**Goal:** Pick a chord's bass note, then strum. Alternate between two bass notes.

**Explain it like this:**
> Pretend you're two players at once: a **bass player** (boom!) and a **strummer** (chuck!). Pick the lowest note of the chord on beat 1, then strum the rest on beat 2. Then pick a different bass note on beat 3 and strum on beat 4. Boom, chuck, boom, chuck. This is how country and folk guitar sound so full.

**Must-know facts:**
- The first bass note is the chord's **root** (its name note): G = string 6 fret 3; C = string 5 fret 3; D = open string 4; A = open string 5; E = open string 6; Am = open string 5.
- The alternate bass is usually the next string over (often the chord's 5th). For example, C: string 5 fret 3, then string 4 fret 2; G: string 6 fret 3, then string 4 open.
- Source: alternate bass picking (*The Only Basic Guitar Instruction Book*) and bass-chord accompaniment (*Alfred's*).

**Graphics:**
- A Neck Map with the **root** shown as a vermillion square and the alternate bass as a green circle. Strum zone shaded.
- A two-lane Strum Grid: "Bass" lane (dot on 1 and 3) and "Strum" lane (arrows on 2 and 4).

**Play it:** "Red River Valley" (G, C, D7) in boom-chuck.
**Check it:** 8 bars at 80 BPM.

### L3.3 — Seventh Chords: E7, A7, G7, C7, B7
**Thread:** Chords · **Time:** 20 min (can be split in two) · **Prereqs:** L2.5, L1.5

**Goal:** Play the five common open dominant-7th chords.

**Explain it like this:**
> A 7th chord is a regular chord plus one extra "spicy" note. It makes the chord want to move somewhere, like D7 wanting to go to G. Blues music is full of 7th chords.

**Must-know facts:**
- **E7** = `0 2 0 1 0 0`, fingers [2] on string 5, fret 2 and [1] on string 3, fret 1. (It's E with one finger lifted.)
- **A7** = `x 0 2 0 2 0`, fingers [2] on string 4, fret 2 and [3] on string 2, fret 2. (It's A with the middle finger lifted.)
- **G7** = `3 2 0 0 0 1`, fingers [3] on string 6, fret 3; [2] on string 5, fret 2; [1] on string 1, fret 1.
- **C7** = `x 3 2 3 1 0`, it's C plus [4] on string 3, fret 3.
- **B7** = `x 2 1 2 0 2`, fingers [2] on string 5, fret 2; [1] on string 4, fret 1; [3] on string 3, fret 2; [4] on string 1, fret 2.
- A dominant 7th chord adds the **♭7** (the note a whole step below the root's octave) to a major chord.
- Pulls: E7 → A, A7 → D, G7 → C, C7 → F, B7 → E, D7 → G (each "wants" to go to the chord a 4th above).

**Graphics:**
- "Before/after" chord boxes (E → E7, A → A7, C → C7), with the changed finger blinking.
- A "pull arrow" chart: each 7th chord with an arrow to its home chord.

**Play it:** `E | E | A7 | E | B7 | A7 | E | B7` (a taste of the blues), quarter notes.
**Check it:** Microphone check of all 5 chords, plus "Which chord does G7 want to go to?" → C.

### L3.4 — Sus and Add Chords: Pretty Shapes
**Thread:** Chords · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L3.3

**Goal:** Play sus2, sus4, and add9 shapes for color and movement.

**Explain it like this:**
> "Sus" is short for **suspended**, like a ball hanging in the air. A sus chord swaps the 3rd (the happy/sad note) for a different note, so the chord sounds **neither happy nor sad**. It's floating! Then you drop back to the normal chord and it lands.

**Must-know facts:**
- **Asus2** `x 0 2 2 0 0` · **Asus4** `x 0 2 2 3 0` · **Dsus2** `x x 0 2 3 0` · **Dsus4** `x x 0 2 3 3` · **Esus4** `0 2 2 2 0 0`.
- **Cadd9** `x 3 2 0 3 0` (C chord plus a D on string 2). **Em7** `0 2 2 0 3 0`, a fuller version than L1.1's one-finger shape.
- sus2 replaces the 3rd with the 2nd. sus4 replaces it with the 4th. "add9" keeps the 3rd and adds the 9th (= the 2nd, an octave up).
- Classic move: D → Dsus4 → D → Dsus2 → D (one hand position with fingers lifting and adding).

**Graphics:** Note-role colors: the 3rd (sky blue) fades out and a gray "2" or "4" dot fades in. A hanging-ball animation for "suspended."

**Play it:** `G | Cadd9 | Em7 | Dsus4 D` (a modern acoustic-pop progression).
**Check it:** Ear question: "Did the chord resolve?" plus play the D-sus dance.

### L3.5 — Rests and Ties: Playing the Silence
**Thread:** Rhythm/Reading · **Time:** 12 min · **Prereqs:** L3.1

**Goal:** Read rests and ties and play them cleanly.

**Explain it like this:**
> Music is made of sound **and** silence. A **rest** means "be quiet now." On guitar, strings keep ringing unless you stop them, so you have to mute them on purpose. A **tie** is a little curved line that glues two notes together, so you hold the first one and **don't** play the second.

**Must-know facts:**
- Rest values: whole (4 beats), half (2), quarter (1), eighth (½).
- Muting for rests: relax fretting-hand pressure (keep fingers touching the strings), and/or lay the picking hand's palm on the strings.
- A tied strum becomes a ghost strum in the Strum Grid (the hand keeps moving).
- A dot after a note adds half its value (dotted half = 3 beats; dotted quarter = 1½).

**Graphics:**
- A rest-symbol chart with beat lengths. Each rest has a "shh" icon showing how to mute.
- A tie drawn across two Strum Grid boxes: the second arrow turns into a ghost.

**Try it:** Stop-start rhythm drills ("strum-strum-REST-strum").
**Check it:** Read and play 4 rhythm cards correctly.

### L3.6 — Alternate Picking and the 1-2-3-4 Warm-Up
**Thread:** Technique · **Time:** 12 min · **Prereqs:** L1.6, L2.9

**Goal:** Pick single notes down-up and build finger independence.

**Explain it like this:**
> When you pick fast, going only down is like running with one leg. **Alternate picking** means down, up, down, up, using both directions so you never waste a move. The 1-2-3-4 exercise trains each finger to do its own job.

**Must-know facts:**
- **Chromatic warm-up:** on each string, play frets 1-2-3-4 with fingers 1-2-3-4 using alternate picking. Then move to the next string. Go across all 6 and back. (This appears in Learn & Master, Great Courses Lessons 2–3, and *Guitar Exercises For Dummies*.)
- Leave fingers down once they're placed when going up the frets (they help the next finger). Keep fingers hovering close.
- Metronome ladder: start at 60 BPM eighth notes and earn +5 BPM after 3 clean runs (*Speed Building Handbook* approach).

**Graphics:** A Neck Map grid of 24 dots (frets 1–4, all strings) in finger colors. A pick-direction lane under the tab (⊓ down / V up).

**Try it:** Warm-up at a personal tempo. The Tempo Ladder records your best.
**Check it:** One full up-and-back run at 70 BPM eighths, clean.

### L3.8 — Notes on Strings 5 and 6: The Full First-Position Map
**Thread:** Notes/Melody · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L3.7

**Must-know facts:**

- String 6 (E): **E** open, **F** fret 1 [1], **G** fret 3 [3].

### L3.9 — Sharps and Flats in First Position
**Thread:** Notes · **Time:** 10 min · **Prereqs:** L3.8, L2.9

**Must-know facts:**
- A sharp = one fret higher. A flat = one fret lower.
- Common first-position sharps/flats: F♯ (string 1 fret 2 / string 4 fret 4 / string 6 fret 2), C♯ (string 2 fret 2 / string 5 fret 4), G♯ (string 1 fret 4 / string 3 fret 1), B♭ (string 3 fret 3 / string 5 fret 1).
- **Open-string flat rule:** to flat an open-string note, go to the next lower string and play fret 4 (for example, E♭ = string 2 fret 4). The exception is string 3 → string 2: the G string's B is at fret 4, so B♭ is at G string fret 3. (From Learn & Master Session 4.)
- In written music, a sharp or flat lasts for the rest of the measure. A natural sign (♮) cancels it.

**Graphics:** The First-Position Map adds sharps/flats as smaller split-color dots ("F♯ / G♭"). A piano strip above for orientation.

**Play it:** "Minuet in G" (first section) or a riff with F♯.
**Check it:** 6 find-the-note questions.

### L3.10 — Tuning by Ear: The 5th-Fret Method
**Thread:** Ear/Technique · **Time:** 12 min · **Prereqs:** L3.8, L0.4

**Goal:** Tune the guitar to itself without a tuner.

**Explain it like this:**
> If one string is right, it can help tune the others. Press string 6 at fret 5 and you get an A, the same note as open string 5. Make them match! Keep doing that down the line. There's one trick: the G string uses **fret 4** to match the B string.

**Must-know facts:**
- 6th string fret 5 = open 5th (A). 5th fret 5 = open 4th (D). 4th fret 5 = open 3rd (G). **3rd fret 4 = open 2nd (B).** 2nd fret 5 = open 1st (E).
- Always adjust the **open** string, not the fretted one.
- When two notes are almost the same, you hear a slow "wah-wah" wobble (beats). Tune until it stops.

**Graphics:** A Neck Map with 5 matching pairs connected by arcs (4 arcs at fret 5, one highlighted arc at fret 4). An audio "beating" demo.

**Try it:** The app detunes a virtual guitar and you match pairs by ear, then do it on your real guitar and check with the tuner.
**Check it:** Tune all strings by ear, then the tuner confirms all are within ±15 cents.

### L3.11 — Chord Families and the Magic 1–4–5
**Thread:** Theory · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L3.3

**Goal:** Know that songs use a **family** of chords, and the most important ones are numbered 1, 4, and 5.

**Explain it like this:**
> Chords live in families called **keys**. Every family has a leader, the **1 chord**, which feels like home. Two best friends always come along: the **4 chord** and the **5 chord**. The 5 chord especially wants to lead you back home to 1. Thousands of songs use just 1, 4, and 5!

**Must-know facts:**
- I–IV–V in the keys you can already play:
  - **G**: G, C, D (D7)
  - **C**: C, F, G (G7). F comes in Level 4. Use Fmaj7 `x x 3 2 1 0` for now.
  - **D**: D, G, A (A7)
  - **A**: A, D, E (E7)
  - **E**: E, A, B7
- Roman numerals: capital = major (I, IV, V). The family also has a **minor 6 chord** (vi): Em in G, Am in C, Bm in D (Level 4), F♯m in A, C♯m in E.
- Chords are numbered by counting up the letters from the key's name: in G, G=1, A=2, B=3, C=4, D=5, E=6. (The full scale and why come in L4.10.)

**Graphics:**
- **Number Wheel**: a ring for each key, with 1 at the top and 4 and 5 highlighted as "best friends" (warm color = major, cool = minor).
- A "count up the letters" ladder animation for the key of G.

**Try it:** Play 1–4–5–1 in all five keys.
**Check it:** "In the key of D, what is the 5 chord?" → A. (5 questions)

### L3.12 — Ear Lab: Hearing Chord Changes
**Thread:** Ear · **Time:** 10 min · **Prereqs:** L3.11

**Goal:** Hear when a chord changes, and whether it moved to 4 or 5.

**Explain it like this:**
> Listen to the **bass** (the lowest sound). When it moves, the chord usually changed. Going to 5 sounds like "uh-oh, we need to go home." Going to 4 sounds like "let's take a little trip."

**Must-know facts:**
- Count bars out loud while listening ("1-2-3-4, 2-2-3-4…") to know where changes happen.
- Common song sections: intro, verse, chorus, bridge, outro.

**Graphics:** A waveform timeline with bar numbers. Tap when the chord changes, and the app shows your taps vs. the real changes.

**Check it:** Identify 1/4/5 in 6 short clips (5 correct).

# LEVEL 4 — Movable Shapes
**Big goal:** Break free of the first three frets. Learn power chords, barre chords, and capo, and find any root note. Learn the major scale, keys, and the Number System that explains every song chart.
**Time:** 6–8 weeks (barre chords take patience. Learn & Master estimates 2–3 weeks for each barre family.) · **Level Song:** "House of the Rising Sun" (Am, C, D, F, E)

---

### L4.1 — Notes on the 6th String (the Whole Way Up)
**Thread:** Notes · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L3.9

**Explain it like this:**
> Barre chords and power chords get their names from the note under your first finger on the fattest string. So we need a map of string 6. Use the dots on your guitar as landmarks: fret 5 is A, fret 7 is B, fret 12 is E again.

**Must-know facts:**
- String 6 natural notes: **E (0), F (1), G (3), A (5), B (7), C (8), D (10), E (12)**.
- Sharps/flats fill the gaps (F♯/G♭ 2, G♯/A♭ 4, A♯/B♭ 6, C♯/D♭ 9, D♯/E♭ 11).
- Everything repeats after fret 12 (fret 15 = G, 17 = A).
- String 1 has the exact same note names as string 6.

**Graphics:** A long horizontal string-6 strip with natural notes as large dots and sharps/flats as small ones. Inlay markers bold. A "landmark" flag on frets 3, 5, 7, 12. "Twin string" animation showing string 1 matching.

**Try it:** "Note Hunt" game: the app names a note and you find it on string 6 (on screen, then on the guitar with the mic).
**Check it:** 10 notes in under 60 seconds with 8+ correct.

### L4.2 — Notes on the 5th String
**Thread:** Notes · **Time:** 12 min · **Prereqs:** L4.1

**Must-know facts:**
- String 5 natural notes: **A (0), B (2), C (3), D (5), E (7), F (8), G (10), A (12)**.
- Shortcut: any note on string 6 is found on string 5 **five frets lower** (same note, same octave). E.g., A on string 6 fret 5 = open A on string 5.

**Graphics:** Strings 5 and 6 maps stacked. Linked same-name notes are connected by thin lines.
**Check it:** Note Hunt on string 5 (8/10), plus mixed strings 5 and 6.

### L4.3 — Power Chords
**Thread:** Chords · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L4.1, L4.2

**Goal:** Play two- and three-note power chords anywhere, rooted on strings 6 and 5.

**Explain it like this:**
> A power chord is a mini chord with only two different notes: the root and the 5th. No 3rd means it's not happy or sad, just **strong**. That's why rock guitarists love it, especially with distortion. It's the same shape everywhere: move it up and down and it changes names.

**Must-know facts:**
- **6th-string root**: finger [1] on string 6 at the root fret, [3] on string 5 two frets higher, (optional) [4] on string 4 two frets higher. Example **G5** = `3 5 5 x x x`.
- **5th-string root**: same shape moved over one string. Example **C5** = `x 3 5 5 x x`.
- Written as letter + 5 (G5, C5, A5…).
- Mute unused strings by letting the underside of finger 1 lightly touch them.
- Source: *Total Rock Guitar* builds from power chords. Learn & Master Session 15.

**Graphics:** A power-chord "stamp" shape (root = vermillion square, 5th = green circles) sliding along the neck, with the name updating from string 6/5 note maps. An X-ray "muting" view.

**Play it:** A rock riff `E5 | G5 A5 | E5 | D5 C5` with downstrokes.
**Check it:** Name-and-play: "Play B5 on string 5" etc. (6 prompts).

### L4.4 — Palm Muting and the Rock Chug
**Thread:** Technique/Rhythm · **Time:** 12 min · **Prereqs:** L4.3

**Explain it like this:**
> Rest the side of your picking hand (the pinky side of your palm) **lightly** on the strings right where they meet the bridge. Now strum. You get a tight "chug" sound instead of a big ring. Too far toward the neck and the notes die completely. Find the sweet spot.

**Must-know facts:**
- Palm-mute marking: "P.M." with a dashed line.
- Edge of palm on the strings at or just in front of the bridge saddles.
- Combine with downstroke eighth notes for rock rhythm. Accent unmuted hits for punch.

**Graphics:** A close-up of the hand position with a "sweet zone" band drawn over the bridge. A sound slider shows open → muted → dead.

**Play it:** Palm-muted eighth-note power-chord riff with accents on beat 1.
**Check it:** Microphone detects muted vs. open hits in a call-and-response drill.

### L4.5 — Getting Ready for Barre Chords: Fmaj7 and the Mini-F
**Thread:** Chords · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L3.11

**Explain it like this:**
> The F chord is the big hill every guitarist climbs. We'll climb it in small steps. First Fmaj7 (no barre at all!), then a little F where your first finger only flattens over **two** strings.

**Must-know facts:**
- **Fmaj7** = `x x 3 2 1 0`, fingers [3] on string 4, fret 3; [2] on string 3, fret 2; [1] on string 2, fret 1.
- **Mini-F** = `x x 3 2 1 1`, finger [1] flat across strings 1–2 at fret 1, plus [2] on string 3, fret 2 and [3] on string 4, fret 3.
- Barre tips: roll finger 1 slightly onto its bony (thumb-facing) side; keep it close to the fret; thumb lower on the back of the neck; pull back gently with the arm instead of squeezing with the thumb.

**Graphics:** A barre "bar" drawn as a rounded blue capsule over the strings. A side-view showing the finger rolled onto its side.

**Play it:** `C | Fmaj7 | C | G` → then replace Fmaj7 with mini-F.
**Check it:** Mini-F with both barred strings ringing (mic check: string-by-string).

### L4.6 — The Full F Barre Chord (E-Shape)
**Thread:** Chords · **Time:** 20 min (repeat over several days) · **Prereqs:** L4.5, L2.4

**Explain it like this:**
> Your first finger becomes a **capo** (a clamp across all six strings). The other three fingers make an **E chord shape** in front of it. That's why it's called an **E-shape barre chord**: it's an E chord, moved up!

**Must-know facts:**
- **F** = `1 3 3 2 1 1`: [1] barres all 6 strings at fret 1, [3] on string 5 fret 3, [4] on string 4 fret 3, [2] on string 3 fret 2.
- **Practice tip:** barre chords are easier higher up the neck (less string tension). Start at fret 5 (that's A major), then work down to fret 1.
- Build-up drill: barre only → add one finger at a time → the full chord. Check string by string.
- Expect this to take weeks, not days. That is normal.

**Graphics:**
- An animation: the E chord with fingers renumbered (1→2, 2→3, 3→4) slides up one fret while the "capo finger" barre drops in behind it.
- The root (string 6) is marked with a vermillion square so the name connects to the string-6 note map.

**Play it:** `C | F | G | C` → "Jingle Bells" full chorus strumming (C, F, G7) from L2.10.
**Check it:** All 6 strings ring on F at fret 1 or 3 (mic check), plus the progression at 70 BPM.

### L4.7 — Moving the E-Shape: Major, Minor, 7, m7
**Thread:** Chords · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L4.6, L4.1

**Must-know facts (shown at fret 1 = F, movable anywhere):**
- Major `1 3 3 2 1 1` · **minor** `1 3 3 1 1 1` (lift finger 2) · **7** `1 3 1 2 1 1` (lift finger 4) · **m7** `1 3 1 1 1 1`.
- The chord name = the note under finger 1 on string 6. For example, at fret 5 = A, at fret 3 = G, at fret 7 = B.
- These match open E, Em, E7, and Em7 (L2, L3) moved up.

**Explain it like this:**
> One shape, 12 chords! Wherever your barre goes, the note on the fat string tells you the chord's name. Lift one finger, and major becomes minor.

**Graphics:** A "shape family" card showing all 4 boxes with note-role colors (the moving 3rd and 7th light up). A fret slider that renames the chord live.

**Play it:** `Am (5) | F (1) | C (8) | G (3)`, all E-shape barres.
**Check it:** "Play Gm," "Play B7," "Play C♯m" (6 prompts).

### L4.8 — The A-Shape Barre (Root on String 5)
**Thread:** Chords · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L4.7, L4.2

**Explain it like this:**
> Same idea as the E-shape, but now the chord is an **A shape** moved up, and its name comes from the note on **string 5**. With both shapes, you can play any chord in two places.

**Must-know facts (shown at fret 2 = B, movable):**
- **Major** `x 2 4 4 4 2`: [1] barre at fret 2, fingers [2][3][4] on strings 4, 3, 2 at fret 4. **Or** a ring-finger mini-barre across strings 4–2. String 1 may be muted if the ring finger blocks it.
- **minor** `x 2 4 4 3 2`: [1] barre, [3] on string 4, [4] on string 3, [2] on string 2 fret 3. (**Bm** is the classic first one.)
- **7** `x 2 4 2 4 2` · **m7** `x 2 4 2 3 2`.
- Name = the note under finger 1 on string 5.
- Learn & Master Session 8 teaches the 5th-string barre right after the 6th-string barre.

**Graphics:** Morph animation from open A / Am / A7 / Am7 to the barred versions. A combined neck view: "same chord, two places" (for example, C at fret 8 E-shape and fret 3 A-shape).

**Play it:** `Bm | G | D | A` (key of D with a real Bm), then any 1–5–6–4 progression using both shapes.
**Check it:** Clean Bm (mic), plus 6 "play this chord with the A-shape" prompts.

### L4.9 — The Capo: Change Keys Instantly
**Thread:** Chords/Theory · **Time:** 12 min · **Prereqs:** L4.6, L2.9

**Explain it like this:**
> A capo is a clamp that acts like a moving **nut**. Put it on fret 2 and every open-chord shape you know sounds **two half steps higher**. You keep playing easy shapes, and the song moves to a new key, often a better one for singing.

**Must-know facts:**
- Real chord = shape name + number of frets (half steps). **Capo 2 + G shape = A.** **Capo 3 + C shape = D♯/E♭.** **Capo 5 + D shape = G.**
- Place the capo just behind the fret (like a finger), straight across, snug but not crushing. Re-check tuning after clamping.
- Fret numbers in chord boxes are **relative to the capo** (the capo becomes "fret 0").
- Great Courses Lesson 18 and *How I Play Guitar* both use capo for new keys *and* new textures.

**Graphics:** A Neck Map with a capo graphic. Chord boxes show "shape" in gray and "sounds like" in bold. A capo calculator widget (shape + fret → real chord).

**Try it:** Play `G | C | D` with capo 0, 2, 4, and 7. Hear the key rise.

### L4.10 — The Major Scale: Whole, Whole, Half, Whole, Whole, Whole, Half
**Thread:** Theory/Notes · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L2.9, L3.8

**Goal:** Build any major scale from its recipe and play it on one string and in first position.

**Explain it like this:**
> A major scale is a ladder of 8 notes that sounds like "Do-Re-Mi-Fa-Sol-La-Ti-Do." Every major scale uses the **same recipe** of steps: **Whole, Whole, Half, Whole, Whole, Whole, Half** (W W H W W W H). Start on any note, follow the recipe, and you get that note's major scale.

**Must-know facts:**
- C major: C D E F G A B C (no sharps or flats: the half steps fall on E–F and B–C naturally).
- G major: G A B C D E **F♯** G. D major: D E **F♯** G A B **C♯** D.
- **Rule:** every letter is used exactly once per scale (that's why the 7th note of F major is E, and the 4th is **B♭**, not A♯).
- On guitar: play the recipe **on one string** (for example, G major on string 3 from open G: 0-2-4-5-7-9-11-12) to *see* the steps. This is how Great Courses Lesson 4 teaches it.
- First-position C major (from L3.8) and a movable one-octave major-scale shape rooted on string 6, with the root under finger 2: string 6 fingers 2-4, string 5 fingers 1-2-4, string 4 fingers 1-3-4. For example, G major at fret 3: string 6 frets 3-5, string 5 frets 2-3-5, string 4 frets 2-4-5.

**Graphics:**
- A **staircase** graphic: W steps are tall, H steps are short.
- A single-string Neck Map with hops labeled W/W/H/W/W/W/H.
- A movable one-octave pattern in note-role colors (root squares at both ends).

**Try it:** Build G, D, A, and F major on paper (drag-the-letters widget), then play them on one string.
**Check it:** Build 3 scales correctly, plus play G major on one string to a drone.

### L4.11 — Keys and Key Signatures (Just What You Need)
**Thread:** Theory · **Time:** 12 min · **Prereqs:** L4.10

**Explain it like this:**
> A **key** is a family of 7 notes (from its major scale) that a song mostly uses. The **key signature** is a little "cheat sheet" at the start of sheet music that says which notes are always sharp or flat.

**Must-know facts:**
- Common guitar keys: C (none), G (F♯), D (F♯ C♯), A (F♯ C♯ G♯), E (F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯), F (B♭).
- Order of sharps: F C G D A E B. Order of flats: B E A D G C F.
- Quick trick (Learn & Master): with sharps, the key is a half step **above the last sharp**. With flats, the key is the **next-to-last flat** (F major, with one flat, is memorized).

**Graphics:** A "key card" for each common key: the scale letters, its key signature, and a guitar-friendliness meter (open-string-friendly keys glow).

**Check it:** 6 questions ("What key has 2 sharps?" → D).

### L4.12 — The Number System: Every Chord Has a Jersey Number
**Thread:** Theory · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L4.10, L3.11

**Goal:** Build the 7 chords of any major key and read progressions as numbers.

**Explain it like this:**
> Build a chord on every note of the major scale, using only notes from the scale, and you get 7 chords. They're always the same types, in every key: **1 major, 2 minor, 3 minor, 4 major, 5 major, 6 minor, 7 diminished** (the 7 is rare). So musicians just say numbers. "It's 1–5–6–4 in G!" means G–D–Em–C.

**Must-know facts:**
- Chord pattern in every major key: **I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii°** (upper case = major).
- Key of C: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, B°. Key of G: G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F♯°. Key of D: D, Em, F♯m, G, A, Bm, C♯°.
- How a chord is built: stack every other scale note (1-3-5). Major = 4 half steps + 3. Minor = 3 + 4. Diminished = 3 + 3.
- Famous progressions (from the *Progressions: Classics* series): **1–4–5**, **1–5–6–4**, **1–6–4–5**, **6–4–1–5**, **2–5–1**.
- **Transposing** = keep the numbers and change the key.
- Source: *Music Theory for the Music Industry* (Nashville Number System).

**Graphics:**
- **Number Wheel** for each key: 7 chord tiles, colored major (warm) / minor (cool) / diminished (gray), each with a jersey number.
- A "transpose" slider: the numbers stay and the letters change.
- A stacked-thirds tower for building chords (note-role colors: R, 3, 5).

**Try it:** Play 1–5–6–4 in G, C, D, and A (with barre chords where needed).
**Check it:** 8 questions ("6 chord in D?" → Bm; "Transpose 1–4–5 from G to A" → A, D, E).
