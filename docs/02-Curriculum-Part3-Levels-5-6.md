# FretStudy Curriculum — Part 3: Levels 5–6

*From here on, scale and lead lessons use **Note-Role colors** by default (Root = vermillion square, 3rd = sky blue, 5th = green, 7th = purple, others = gray, blue note ♭5 = blue diamond). Learners can always flip the label toggle to Fingers or Note Names.*

Neck positions are written as **string:fret** (for example, `6:5` = string 6, fret 5).

# LEVEL 5 — Lead Guitar Basics
**Big goal:** Play your first solos. Learn the minor pentatonic "Box 1," the techniques that make guitar sing (hammer-ons, pull-offs, slides, bends, vibrato), and the 12-bar blues, the training ground for improvising.
**Time:** 6–8 weeks (Learn & Master gives pentatonic scales 4 weeks on their own) · **Level Song:** "First Blues": a 12-bar shuffle in A, with one chorus of rhythm and one chorus of your own solo.

---

### L5.1 — The Minor Pentatonic Scale: Box 1
**Thread:** Notes/Lead · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L4.1, L3.6

**Explain it like this:**
> "Penta" means five. The pentatonic scale is a **5-note ladder**. It's the scale behind most rock and blues solos. It leaves out the two notes that are easiest to "bump into" wrong, so almost anything you play sounds good. Its shape looks like a box, with two notes on every string.

**Must-know facts:**
- Formula: **1, ♭3, 4, 5, ♭7**. A minor pentatonic = **A C D E G**.
- **Box 1 at fret 5** (A minor):
  - String 6: 5 (A, root), 8 (C)
  - String 5: 5 (D), 7 (E)
  - String 4: 5 (G), 7 (A, root)
  - String 3: 5 (C), 7 (D)
  - String 2: 5 (E), 8 (G)
  - String 1: 5 (A, root), 8 (C)
- Fingering: finger 1 plays every fret-5 note. Fingers 3 and 4 play the fret-7 and fret-8 notes.
- Movable: the root on string 6 names the scale (fret 8 = C minor pentatonic, fret 12 = E minor pentatonic, open/fret 0 shape for E minor).

**Graphics:**
- Neck Map frets 4–9, root squares glowing. A toggle shows intervals (1, ♭3, 4, 5, ♭7).
- An animated "up the ladder" playthrough with tab below.
- "Box" outline drawn around the shape.

**Try it:** Ascend and descend with alternate picking at 60 BPM → Tempo Ladder.
**Check it:** Play the box up and down from memory (mic check), plus "Which notes are roots?"

### L5.2 — Pentatonic Licks and Patterns
**Thread:** Lead/Technique · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L5.1

**Explain it like this:**
> Scales are like the alphabet. **Licks** are like words. Learn a few words and you can start making sentences (solos).

**Must-know facts:**
- Sequences: groups of 3 ("1-2-3, 2-3-4…"), groups of 4, and "up two strings, back one."
- 5 starter licks in Box 1 (original, app-written), each ending on a root.
- Research on jazz improvisers (Solstad, *Strategies in Jazz Guitar Improvisation*) shows that players build solos from memorized "chunks." Learning short licks is how improvisation grows.

**Graphics:** Each lick shows a Neck Map with a numbered path (1, 2, 3…) plus tab, at slow and full speed.
**Check it:** Play 3 licks over a backing track in A.

### L5.3 — The 12-Bar Blues
**Thread:** Chords/Theory · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L3.3, L3.11

**Explain it like this:**
> The 12-bar blues is a song "map" that's 12 measures long and uses only the 1, 4, and 5 chords. Once you learn the map, you can jam with anyone, anywhere, because so many blues and rock songs follow it.

**Must-know facts:**
- Standard form (each box = 1 bar):
  `I | I | I | I |`
  `IV | IV | I | I |`
  `V | IV | I | V |` (the last bar is the **turnaround**, going back to the top).
- "Quick change" version: bar 2 goes to IV.
- In blues, all three chords are usually **dominant 7ths**: in A: **A7, D7, E7**. In E: **E7, A7, B7**.
- Blues often counts the form in "choruses" (one time through 12 bars = 1 chorus).

**Graphics:** A 12-box grid in 3 rows with chord numbers and colors (I = vermillion, IV = blue, V = green). A playhead lights each bar with the backing track. A toggle switches between "numbers" and "letters in A / E / G."

**Try it:** Strum it in E and A with quarter notes.
**Check it:** Fill in an empty 12-bar grid (drag numbers), plus play one chorus in A.

### L5.4 — Shuffle Feel and the Boogie Riff
**Thread:** Rhythm · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L5.3, L4.3

**Explain it like this:**
> In a shuffle, eighth notes aren't even: they go **long-short, long-short**, like "HUMP-ty DUMP-ty" or a galloping horse. Count "1-trip-let, 2-trip-let" and play on the "1" and the "let."

**Must-know facts:**
- Swing/shuffle eighths = the first and third notes of a triplet (a 2:1 long-short feel).
- **Boogie riff** (5–6 shuffle) in A: alternate the power chord **A5** (`x 0 2 x x x`) with **A6** (`x 0 4 x x x`, pinky on string 4, fret 4). Same for D (strings 4–3) and E (strings 6–5).
- Palm mute lightly for that classic sound.

**Graphics:** A triplet Strum Grid (`1 trip let 2 trip let…`) with hits on "1" and "let." A boogie-riff Neck Map animation with fingers 1 and 3 planted and finger 4 reaching.

**Play it:** The full 12-bar in A with the boogie riff.
**Check it:** One chorus at 90 BPM with a swung feel (the app checks the swing ratio).

### L5.5 — Hammer-Ons and Pull-Offs
**Thread:** Technique · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L5.1

**Explain it like this:**
> A **hammer-on** is when you pick one note, then slam another finger down like a tiny hammer to make a second note without picking again. A **pull-off** is the opposite: pick a note, then flick your finger off the string sideways so the lower note rings. Two notes, one pick. Smooth!

**Must-know facts:**
- Tab: `5h7` (hammer-on), `7p5` (pull-off). A curved slur line appears in standard notation.
- Hammer close to the fret with the fingertip. For a pull-off, pull slightly *down* (toward the floor), plucking the string. Don't just lift.
- The lower note's finger must already be down before the pull-off.
- Together these are called **legato** (smooth playing).

**Graphics:** A slow-motion side view of the hammer and pull "flick." Tab with H/P arcs that glow as they sound.

**Try it:** Hammer and pull every string of Box 1.
**Check it:** The mic hears 2 notes from 1 pick on 4 of 5 attempts.

### L5.6 — Slides
**Thread:** Technique · **Time:** 10 min · **Prereqs:** L5.5

**Must-know facts:**
- Tab: `5/7` (slide up), `7\5` (slide down). Keep pressure as you slide so the note keeps ringing.
- A slide into a note from "nowhere" (1–2 frets below) is a common blues and country move.
- Slides help **move between boxes** later (L6.3).

**Explain it like this:**
> Keep your finger pressed down and glide it along the string like a sled. The sound slides with you.

**Graphics:** A finger-glide animation with a ribbon trail. Tab slashes animate.
**Check it:** 3 slide licks recognized by the mic.

### L5.7 — String Bends (In Tune!)
**Thread:** Technique · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L5.1

**Explain it like this:**
> Bending is pushing the string up (toward your face) so the note goes higher, like stretching a rubber band. It makes the guitar sound like a singing voice. The trick is to bend to the **right note**, not just "somewhere higher."

**Must-know facts:**
- **Whole-step bend** = the note rises 2 frets' worth. **Half-step** = 1 fret.
- Use the 3rd finger with fingers 1 and 2 behind it on the same string helping push. Turn the wrist (like turning a doorknob) instead of pushing with one fingertip.
- **Check pitch:** play the target note 2 frets up first, then bend to match it.
- **The 3 sweet bend spots in A minor pentatonic Box 1** (*Spotlight Blues Guitar*):
  1. `3:7` (D) → bend a whole step to **E** (4 → 5)
  2. `2:8` (G) → bend a whole step to **A** (♭7 → root)
  3. `1:8` (C) → bend a whole step to **D** (♭3 → 4), or just a quarter bend for a bluesy "curl"
- Electric guitars bend easiest (lighter strings). On acoustic, try half-step bends first.

**Graphics:** The Neck Map shows the string curving, with an arrow and the **target dot** (dashed outline) 2 frets up. A live **pitch meter** shows how close the bend is to the target.

**Try it:** Bend-and-match drill: play the target, then bend to it.
**Check it:** 3 bends that land within ±20 cents of the target.

### L5.8 — Vibrato
**Thread:** Technique · **Time:** 10 min · **Prereqs:** L5.7

**Explain it like this:**
> Vibrato is a little wobble on a note, like a singer's voice shaking at the end of a long note. It makes notes sound alive instead of flat.

**Must-know facts:**
- Rock/blues vibrato: small, repeated bends and releases using the wrist (a doorknob motion). Classical vibrato: rocking along the string's length.
- Steady speed matters more than width. Try matching it to the song's beat (for example, 8th-note pulses).

**Graphics:** A note "wave" visual (flat line vs. wavy line), with a slider for vibrato width and speed.
**Check it:** Hold a note for 2 beats with even vibrato (the mic sees regular pitch wobble).

### L5.9 — The Blues Scale and Blue Notes
**Thread:** Notes/Theory · **Time:** 12 min · **Prereqs:** L5.1

**Explain it like this:**
> Add one extra "crunchy" note to the minor pentatonic and it becomes the **blues scale**. That extra note sits right between two others. Slide through it. Don't park on it!

**Must-know facts:**
- Blues scale = **1, ♭3, 4, ♭5, 5, ♭7**. In A: **A C D E♭ E G**.
- In Box 1, the ♭5 (E♭) is at `5:6` and `3:8`.
- "Blue notes" = the ♭3, ♭5, and ♭7 against a major or dominant chord. They create the bluesy tension. (Learn & Master Session 13.)

**Graphics:** Box 1 with the ♭5 drawn as **blue diamonds**. A "pass-through" animation (4 → ♭5 → 5).
**Check it:** Play the blues scale up and down plus one lick using the ♭5 as a passing note.

### L5.10 — Improvising: Make Your Own Solo
**Thread:** Lead/Ear · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L5.2, L5.7, L5.3

**Explain it like this:**
> A good solo is like telling a story. Say a short idea, take a breath, then answer it. You don't need lots of notes. You need **good** notes and some empty space.

**Must-know facts:**
- **Start tiny** (Spotlight's ear-training approach): solo using 1 note → 2 notes → 3 notes, playing with rhythm and feel.
- **Call and response**: play a 2-bar idea, then answer it.
- **Leave space**: rests are part of the solo.
- **Land on roots**: end phrases on the root of the current chord (or the key).
- Learn & Master's rules for a good solo: **melody** (memorable), **balance** (the parts fit together), **say something** (develop one idea instead of "musical mumbling").

**Graphics:** A "Phrase Builder": a 12-bar grid where learners drag licks (L5.2) into bars and hear the result. A "space meter" shows notes vs. rests.

**Try it:** 1-note, 2-note, and 3-note solos over the A shuffle, then free soloing in Box 1.
**Check it:** Record one 12-bar chorus. Self-review checklist: Did I leave space? Did I land on A? Did I repeat and change an idea?

### L5.11 — Ear Lab: Intervals Part 1
**Thread:** Ear · **Time:** 10 min (repeatable) · **Prereqs:** L4.10

**Explain it like this:**
> An **interval** is the distance between two notes. Each distance has its own sound, and famous songs can help you remember them.

**Must-know facts (public-domain reference tunes):**
- Major 2nd: "Happy Birthday" (first change).
- Major 3rd: "When the Saints Go Marching In" (first two notes).
- Minor 3rd: "Greensleeves" (first two notes).
- Perfect 4th: "Here Comes the Bride" (Wagner).
- Perfect 5th: "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star" (first leap).
- Octave: the same letter, higher (L2.8).
- Method from *Train Your Ear*: compare **two intervals at a time** (M3 vs m3, P4 vs P5), and **sing** them.

**Graphics:** Interval "rulers" on the Neck Map (frets between notes), with a play button and a sing-along prompt.
**Check it:** 10 of 12 correct in a 2-choice drill (M3/m3, P4/P5).

### L5.12 — Dominant 7th Barre Chords for the Blues
**Thread:** Chords · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L4.7, L4.8, L5.3

**Must-know facts:**
- E-shape 7 (root on string 6) and A-shape 7 (root on string 5) from Level 4.
- **Play a 12-bar in any key in one spot:** I = E-shape 7 at the root fret. IV = A-shape 7 at the **same fret**. V = A-shape 7 two frets higher. Example in A: A7 (fret 5, E-shape), D7 (fret 5, A-shape), E7 (fret 7, A-shape).
- Short "top-3-string" and "9th chord" blues shapes (preview for the Blues track).

**Explain it like this:**
> With these two shapes, your hand barely moves and you can play the blues in any key. Just put your first finger on the key's name on string 6.

**Graphics:** A 12-bar grid with mini chord boxes in each bar, and a neck view showing the 3 chord positions within 2 frets.
**Check it:** Play 12-bar blues in G and C using barre 7ths.

# LEVEL 6 — The Whole Neck
**Big goal:** Unlock the entire fretboard. Learn the 5 pentatonic shapes, major pentatonic, relative minor, the CAGED system, triads, and major-scale positions. Add 16th-note strumming and fingerpicking.
**Time:** 8–10 weeks · **Level Song:** "Canon in D" (Pachelbel progression), with a fingerpicked arrangement and a top-note melody.

---

### L6.1 — Relative Minor: Same Notes, Different Home
**Thread:** Theory · **Time:** 12 min · **Prereqs:** L4.12, L5.1

**Explain it like this:**
> C major and A minor use the **exact same 7 notes**. They're like two houses on the same street with the same furniture, but a different front door. In C major, C feels like home. In A minor, A feels like home. Every major key has a **relative minor** that lives on its **6th note**.

**Must-know facts:**
- Relative minor = the 6th degree of the major scale (or 3 half steps below the major root). C → Am, G → Em, D → Bm, A → F♯m, E → C♯m, F → Dm.
- Same key signature (L4.11).
- **That's why A minor pentatonic = C major pentatonic**: same 5 notes, A C D E G.

**Graphics:** A two-house illustration with a shared note "furniture" row. The Number Wheel with the 6 chord highlighted as "the minor door."
**Check it:** 6 questions ("relative minor of D?" → Bm).

---

### L6.2 — The Major Pentatonic
**Thread:** Notes/Lead · **Time:** 15 min · **Prereqs:** L6.1

**Explain it like this:**
> Flip the minor pentatonic's home to a different note and it sounds happy and sunny: country, pop, and classic rock solos. Same box shape, different starting point!

**Must-know facts:**
- Formula **1, 2, 3, 5, 6**. G major pentatonic = **G A B D E** (same notes as E minor pentatonic).
- **Shape trick:** to play a major pentatonic, use the minor pentatonic Box 1 shape **3 frets lower** than the major root. For example, A major pentatonic = Box 1 at fret 2 (F♯ minor shape). The major root is under the pinky on string 6.
- Use **major pentatonic over major-key songs**, minor pentatonic over minor and blues.

**Graphics:** The same box drawn twice: the minor home (root on finger 1) vs. the major home (root on finger 4). Root squares move. Interval labels switch between ♭3/♭7 and 2/6.
**Play it:** Solo over `G | C | D | G` with G major pentatonic.
**Check it:** Ear: "Did this solo use major or minor pentatonic?" plus a played lick in A major pentatonic.

### L6.3 — Pentatonic Shapes 2 and 3
**Thread:** Notes/Lead · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L5.1, L5.6

**Explain it like this:**
> The pentatonic notes don't stop at Box 1. They keep going up the neck in **5 shapes that link together like puzzle pieces**. Each box's right side is the next box's left side.

**Must-know facts (A minor):**
- **Shape 2** (frets 7–10): `6:8,10` `5:7,10` `4:7,10` `3:7,9` `2:8,10` `1:8,10`.
- **Shape 3** (frets 9–13): `6:10,12` `5:10,12` `4:10,12` `3:9,12` `2:10,13` `1:10,12`.
- Shared notes link the shapes (Box 1's higher notes = Shape 2's lower notes).
- Use slides (L5.6) to move between shapes.

**Graphics:** A full-neck map with each shape shaded in its own background tint (pale shading only, so the note-role colors stay clear), plus **overlap columns** highlighted. A puzzle-piece animation snaps shapes together.

**Try it:** Box 1 → slide → Shape 2 → slide → Shape 3 and back.
**Check it:** Play Shapes 2 and 3 from memory (mic).

### L6.4 — Pentatonic Shapes 4 and 5: The Whole Neck
**Thread:** Notes/Lead · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L6.3

**Must-know facts (A minor):**
- **Shape 4** (frets 12–15): `6:12,15` `5:12,15` `4:12,14` `3:12,14` `2:13,15` `1:12,15`.
- **Shape 5** (frets 14–17, which repeats an octave lower at frets 2–5): `6:15,17` `5:15,17` `4:14,17` `3:14,17` `2:15,17` `1:15,17`. The low version: `6:3,5` `5:3,5` `4:2,5` `3:2,5` `2:3,5` `1:3,5`.
- After Shape 5 comes Box 1 again at fret 17 (an octave up).
- Move any key: slide the whole pattern so the roots land on the key's note.

**Graphics:** The full-neck A minor pentatonic map with all 5 shapes, plus a key selector (all 12 keys) that slides the map.
**Try it:** Ascend diagonally across shapes with slides.
**Check it:** "Play shape 4 in G minor" style prompts (4 of 5).

### L6.5 — The CAGED System
**Thread:** Chords/Theory · **Time:** 25 min (two sittings) · **Prereqs:** L4.8, L6.4

**Explain it like this:**
> You already know 5 open chord shapes: **C, A, G, E, and D**. Here's the big secret: each one is **movable**, and they line up along the neck in that order, **C-A-G-E-D**, to make one chord in five places. They're also the "skeletons" inside the 5 scale shapes. Learn CAGED and the neck stops being a mystery.

**Must-know facts (C major across the neck):**
- **C shape**: open C (`x 3 2 0 1 0`).
- **A shape**: barre at fret 3 (`x 3 5 5 5 3`).
- **G shape**: frets 5–8 (root `6:8`, `5:7`, `4:5`, `3:5`, `2:5`, `1:8`). Usually played partially.
- **E shape**: barre at fret 8 (`8 10 10 9 8 8`).
- **D shape**: root `4:10`, then `3:12`, `2:13`, `1:12`.
- Then the C shape again at fret 12 (an octave up).
- The order repeats in every key. Only the starting shape changes (for example, G major starts with the E shape at fret 3 → D shape → C shape → A shape → G shape).
- The pentatonic and major scale shapes each "wrap around" one CAGED chord shape (*Guitar Scales in Context*: learn scales around chord-shape anchors).

**Graphics:**
- **A CAGED "train" diagram**: five colored chord-shape outlines along one long neck, with roots as vermillion squares that link the cars.
- A toggle overlay of the matching pentatonic shape inside each chord shape.
- A key slider (all 12 keys).

**Try it:** Play C major in all 5 shapes, low to high, then G major.
**Check it:** "Play an A major with the C shape" (4 prompts), plus identify a shape from a diagram.

### L6.6 — Triads and Inversions on the Top Strings
**Thread:** Chords · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L6.5, L4.12

**Explain it like this:**
> A triad is the smallest real chord: just the 3 notes (root, 3rd, 5th). You can stack them in any order: root on the bottom, 3rd on the bottom, or 5th on the bottom. Each order is called an **inversion**. On the top 3 strings, they make bright little chords that sound great on top of other people's playing.

**Must-know facts (C major on strings 3-2-1):**
- **Root position** (C-E-G from low to high): `3:5 2:5 1:3`.
- **1st inversion** (E-G-C): `3:9 2:8 1:8`.
- **2nd inversion** (G-C-E): `3:0 2:1 1:0` (open), or `3:12 2:13 1:12`.
- Minor triads: lower the 3rd one fret.
- Slash chords name the bass note: **C/E** = C chord with E in the bass (*Music Theory for the Music Industry*; *How I Play Guitar*).

**Graphics:** Three mini Neck Maps of the inversions linked by arrows up the neck, in note-role colors (R/3/5 always labeled, so you can see the order flip).
**Play it:** Strum `C | F | G | C` with triads only, staying near fret 5–8 (inversions, not jumps).
**Check it:** Identify the inversion of 4 triads (from note names or diagrams).

### L6.7 — Major Scale Positions
**Thread:** Notes · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L4.10, L6.5

**Must-know facts:**
- The major scale in 5 positions, each built around a CAGED chord shape (*Teach Yourself to Play Guitar Scales*, Ch. 8; *Guitar Exercises For Dummies*, major scale patterns 1–5).
- Start with 2 positions: the **E-shape position** (root on string 6) and the **A-shape position** (root on string 5).
- Introduce **3-notes-per-string** fingering as an alternative for speed and triplet practice (Learn & Master Session 17).
- Practice sequences in 3rds (C-E, D-F, E-G…) to hear the scale as music, not just a ladder.

**Explain it like this:**
> The pentatonic was a 5-note ladder. The major scale is the full 7-note ladder, the "Do-Re-Mi" one, laid across the neck. Same idea: shapes wrapped around chord skeletons.

**Graphics:** Major scale overlaid on the CAGED chord (chord tones bright, other scale notes gray), with tab.
**Check it:** Play G major in 2 positions with a 3rds sequence at 70 BPM.

- Shifting drill: frets 1–4 → 5–8 → 9–12 on one string, then back.

### L6.9 — Sixteenth-Note Strumming and Accents
**Thread:** Rhythm · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L3.1, L4.4

**Explain it like this:**
> Now the beat splits into **four**: "1-e-and-a, 2-e-and-a." Your hand moves twice as fast (down-up-down-up for each beat), so keep it super loose. Choose which strums are loud (accents) and which are ghosts. That's the secret of funk and pop grooves.

**Must-know facts:**
- 16th note = ¼ beat. Count `1 e & a`. Downs on `1` and `&`, ups on `e` and `a`.
- Accents (>) = louder hits. Ghost or muted strums in between keep the groove.
- Funk "scratch" = muted strings with the fretting hand.

**Graphics:** A 16-box Strum Grid with accent arrows larger. Ghost/muted strums shown as "x" arrows. A "hand speed" metronome at half tempo.
**Play it:** 3 grooves (pop, funk, folk-rock) with E-shape and A-shape barres.
**Check it:** One 16th-note groove at 70 BPM with accents detected.

### L6.10 — Fingerstyle Basics: p-i-m-a
**Thread:** Technique · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L4.13 (arpeggios)

**Explain it like this:**
> Put the pick down! Your picking hand's fingers each get a job. Thumb (**p**) plays the bass strings, 6, 5, and 4. Index (**i**) plays string 3, middle (**m**) string 2, ring (**a**) string 1. It's like each finger has its own lane on a running track.

**Must-know facts:**
- p-i-m-a come from Spanish: *pulgar, índice, medio, anular* (Great Courses; classical tradition).
- Pick from the big knuckle. Wrist relaxed, slightly arched, hand still. Thumb stays slightly forward of the fingers.
- **Free stroke** (finger swings into the air) vs **rest stroke** (finger lands on the next string). Free stroke is used for most accompaniment.
- Patterns: `p-i-m-a`, `p-i-m-i`, `p-a-m-i`, and pinches (`p` + `a` together).
- Learn & Master's keys: relaxed hand, thumb slightly forward, free movement, **go slow**.

**Graphics:** The picking-hand illustration with p/i/m/a in finger colors (white outline = picking hand). "Lanes" over strings 6-4 / 3 / 2 / 1. Each note in the tab is labeled with its letter.
**Play it:** Am–C–D–F (House of the Rising Sun) with `p-i-m-a-m-i`.
**Check it:** 2 patterns clean at 70 BPM (mic).

### L6.11 — Travis Picking (Alternating Thumb)
**Thread:** Technique · **Time:** 20 min · **Prereqs:** L6.10, L3.2

**Explain it like this:**
> Your thumb becomes a drummer that never stops, going back and forth between two bass strings on every beat. Your fingers sneak melody notes in **between** the thumb hits. It sounds like two guitarists at once!

**Must-know facts:**
- Thumb plays steady quarter notes, alternating between the chord's root string and another bass string. For C: string 5 → string 4 → string 5 → string 4 (or 5 → 4 → 6 (fret 3) → 4). For G: string 6 → string 4.
- Fingers play on the "&" (off-beats), often strings 2 and 3, and sometimes a **pinch** (thumb + finger together) on beat 1.
- Named for Merle Travis (Learn & Master Session 10, Great Courses Lesson 12, *The Only Basic Guitar* "folk picking").
- Learn the thumb alone first until it's automatic.

**Graphics:** A two-lane tab: "thumb lane" (steady dots on every beat, gray) and "finger lane" (colored dots on &). A bouncing-ball thumb animation.

### L6.13 — 🎵 Level 6 Song: "Canon in D" (Pachelbel)
**Thread:** Song · **Prereqs:** L6.6, L6.10

**Must-know facts:**
- The progression (public domain): **D – A – Bm – F♯m – G – D – G – A** (1–5–6–3–4–1–4–5).
- Uses Bm (A-shape barre, fret 2) and F♯m (E-shape minor barre, fret 2).
- Arrangement levels: (1) strummed, (2) `p-i-m-a` arpeggios, (3) triads on the top strings that walk down the neck, (4) the famous descending melody played as the top note.

**🏁 Level 6 Check:**
- Play: the Canon fingerpicked (level 2 or higher) at 60 BPM, plus a pentatonic run across 3 shapes in a key the app picks.
- Quiz (10 questions): relative minors, major vs minor pentatonic, CAGED order, inversions, a 16th-note count, p-i-m-a lanes, progression by ear.

> **Core Path complete.** At this point learners have the full foundation. Level 7 deepens harmony and musicianship, and Level 8 lets them specialize.
